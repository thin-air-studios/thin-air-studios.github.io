Which airport is the busiest in the world? The honest answer depends on what you count. By passengers, the ranking most people quote comes from ACI World, the trade body for airports. Its 2025 results put Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport first, with about 106.3 million passengers. This guide gives the full top 50 as Short Final lists it, explains what that number means, and shows where the busiest airports sit.

## What the number counts

ACI World counts passengers. That means every person who uses the airport, whether they arrive, depart or change planes there. A passenger on a connecting flight counts as well. The number tells you how many people pass through an airport in a year, which is a useful measure of how big the terminals and the surrounding system have to be.

It is not a count of planes. A hub with many short domestic flights can have a huge passenger total with a moderate number of landings. A busy freight airport can have many landings and few passengers. One flight carrying 300 passengers adds 300 to the passenger count, but only one landing and one takeoff to a movement count.

Other rankings use aircraft movements, the number of takeoffs and landings. That is a different measure, and the order of the airports changes when you use it, which shows how much the answer depends on the question.

## Reading the top ten

The top of the list is a mix of American and Asian hubs, with a few European airports close behind.

[Atlanta](/airports/katl/) is first, with 106.3 million passengers. Its strength is connections: a large share of its traffic changes planes there, so the same passenger can be counted on arrival and again on departure. Dubai and Tokyo Haneda follow, at 95.2 million and 91.7 million. Dallas/Fort Worth and Shanghai Pudong are close at about 85 million each, and Chicago O'Hare, London Heathrow and Istanbul are in the same band.

Look at the spacing. The top ten are all above 82 million passengers, and the gap between the first and tenth is about 24 million. Below the top ten, the numbers fall in steps, so a small change in one year can move an airport several places.

The list also shows how air travel is spread. By country, the United States has the most airports in the top 50, with 14, and China is next with 11. The 50 airports sit in 24 countries and territories.

## Why the rankings change

The order moves from year to year. Airports build new terminals, open or close routes, and see demand grow or fall. Airlines move their hubs. Travel restrictions, economic shocks and weather can all shift a year's numbers, so a single ranking is a snapshot of one year, not a permanent fact.

That is why the game's list carries its year. It is the 2025 result, and a different year would give a different table. A reader comparing airports should check the year before drawing a conclusion.

## Why this matters for the game

In Short Final, the busiest airports are also the busiest skies. The game's levels use the list to choose where to send you. On Beginner and Easy, the game picks from the quieter end of the top 50. On Hard and Chaos it picks from the top of the list.

Playing the busiest airports also shows the real problems of air traffic control. A big hub has several runways, arrivals in waves, and traffic that must be sequenced. You will feel the same pressure a controller at one of these airports feels every day, in a much smaller way.

## Beyond the top 50

The top 50 are a small part of the world's airports. Short Final's data holds more than 29,000 airports, from the largest hubs to small regional fields, and the search finds any of them by code, name or city. Most of those airports handle far fewer passengers than the ones in the table, and many have a single runway and a handful of flights a day.

That spread matters for anyone who wants to understand air traffic. A busy hub has several runways, many controllers and a steady stream of arrivals. A small airport may have one runway, one controller or none, and its traffic is often the work of a single operator. The principles are the same: planes need a runway, a path and a gap from each other. The scale is what changes.

If you want to see the difference, compare the top of the list with a small airport in the same country. Both maps are built the same way, and the runways are shown in the same form, so the contrast is easy to see.

## How to read the codes

Each row shows two codes: an ICAO code, which is the four-letter code used by controllers and flight plans, and an IATA code, which is the three-letter code printed on tickets and bags. Atlanta is KATL and ATL, for example. The difference between the two, and why both exist, is covered in our guide on IATA and ICAO airport codes.
